# Designing for the Whole Life of a Structure

The first construction cost is only one part of a structure's cost. Long-lived buildings, bridges, and facilities need inspection, cleaning, repairs, and eventually replacement. A durable design considers those future tasks before materials are specified.

## Ask what the asset will face

Record the expected climate, moisture, temperature changes, chemical exposure, and loads. A coastal structure needs a different corrosion strategy from an inland office building. Those assumptions should appear in the design brief so reviewers can challenge them.

## Make maintenance possible

Inspectors need safe access to bearings, joints, roofs, drainage, and mechanical equipment. A detail that is easy to build but impossible to inspect can create years of avoidable cost. Space for access and replacement should be considered alongside structural capacity.

## Compare materials beyond their purchase price

Material selection should include embodied carbon, service life, local availability, repair methods, and end-of-life options. Where two choices perform similarly, the one with a clearer maintenance path may be the better long-term decision.

## Close the loop

The handover package should record the design life, inspection intervals, material specifications, and any assumptions that affect future work. Owners can then plan maintenance instead of reacting to failures.
